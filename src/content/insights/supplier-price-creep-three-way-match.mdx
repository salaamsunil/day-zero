Ask most owner-operators what their food cost is and they can tell you. Ask them whether last week's invoices matched what they actually ordered and received, and it goes quiet. That gap is where the money leaks.

It is rarely one big overcharge. It is a dollar here, a missing case there, a price that crept up two deliveries ago and never came back down. None of it is big enough to argue about at 6am on a delivery dock. All of it adds up.

## What actually happens on the dock

The order, the delivery, and the invoice are three separate documents, and they almost never line up perfectly.

- You order 10 kg of beef mince at last week's price.
- The driver leaves 9 kg, because that is what was on the truck.
- The invoice bills 10 kg, at a price 40 cents a kilo higher than last week.

Every step is normal. The supplier is not running a scam. But you have now paid for a kilo you did not get, at a price you did not agree, and the only person who could have caught it was signing for the delivery with one hand while plating breakfast with the other.

## Where it leaks

There are three quiet leaks, and they compound.

**Short deliveries.** You are billed for what was ordered, not what was dropped. Without a goods-received check against the invoice, the shortfall is invisible.

**Price creep.** Supplier prices drift up between deliveries. A 3 to 5 percent rise on a staple, unremarked, becomes your new baseline. Multiply that across every line, every week.

**Wrong or substituted items.** A different pack size, a substitution at a higher price, a duplicated line on the invoice. Each is small. Each is yours to catch, because nobody else will.

The reason these survive is not laziness. It is that catching them means holding three documents side by side, the purchase order, the goods-received note, and the invoice, and reconciling them line by line, for every delivery, while the kitchen is running. Almost nobody has time to do that by hand. So it does not get done.

## Why it matters more than it looks

A restaurant running at a 30 percent food cost keeps roughly 70 cents of every dollar to cover labour, rent, power, and profit, and profit is the last thing in that line. If price creep and short deliveries quietly add two points to your food cost, you have not lost two percent of revenue. You have lost a chunk of the thin slice of profit at the very end.

That is why "we are busy but not making money" is such a common and maddening place to be. The dining room is full. The leak is on the loading dock.

## What to check this week

You do not need software to start. You need one habit.

1. **Reconcile three documents, not one.** For your next five deliveries, put the order, the delivery docket, and the invoice next to each other. Check quantity against what was received, and price against what you last paid. Flag anything over a threshold you set (5 percent is a sensible start).
2. **Track price per unit, not invoice totals.** Totals hide creep. Price per kilo, per litre, per case is where you see it.
3. **Give the person signing for deliveries a count sheet.** If they check quantity at the door, short deliveries stop being invisible.
4. **Keep the flagged ones.** A short list of "billed wrong" items is your evidence when you call the supplier, and suppliers correct quickly when you can show them the line.

Do this for two weeks and you will have a number: what you were being overbilled, in dollars. It is almost always bigger than people expect.

## The point

Your food cost report tells you the leak exists. It does not tell you where. The where is on the dock, in the difference between what you ordered, what you got, and what you paid. Close that gap and you are not cutting quality or raising prices. You are just keeping the margin you already earned.
