<h2><i class="fas fa-bolt"></i> The queue is not all demand</h2>
<p class="section-intro">A line outside your truck looks like a good problem. Part of it is. But past a certain length, the line stops adding sales and starts subtracting them, and you never see the customers who looked, counted heads, and kept walking.</p>
<p style="font-size:0.92rem;color:var(--dark);line-height:1.7">A no-show is a customer who ordered and did not collect. It costs you the food. A walk-off is a customer who wanted to order and left because the wait looked too long. It costs you the whole sale, and you have no record it ever happened. In a two-hour lunch window, walk-offs are almost always the bigger number, and the one nobody measures.</p>
<div class="formula-block">
<div class="formula">Lost revenue = walk-offs &times; average order value</div>
<p>The walk-offs you never counted are usually the biggest line item you are not tracking</p>
</div>
</div>

<div class="tip-box tip-box-mint" style="margin-top:1.5rem"><strong>Why orders-per-hour matters more than it looks:</strong> a truck's revenue is capped by the service window, not the day. If you can serve 30 orders an hour and the rush is 90 minutes, your ceiling is 45 orders, no matter how many people show up. Lifting throughput is often worth more than lifting price.</div>

<h2><i class="fas fa-triangle-exclamation"></i> Where truck margin actually leaks</h2>
<p class="section-intro">Five causes account for most of it. Each has a practical fix.</p>
<div style="font-size:0.9rem;color:var(--dark);line-height:1.8">
<p><strong>1. The window is the bottleneck, not the grill.</strong> Taking the order, repeating it, handling cash, and calling names all happen at the same hatch. Fix: move ordering off the window. When customers order on their phone, the hatch does one job, handover.</p>
<p style="margin-top:1rem"><strong>2. Walk-offs at peak.</strong> A long line turns demand away silently. Fix: show a live wait time so people commit and order ahead instead of leaving, and cook to the queue rather than to whoever is at the front.</p>
<p style="margin-top:1rem"><strong>3. Aggregator commissions.</strong> 25&ndash;35% off the top of every delivery order is often more than your entire net margin. Fix: own the ordering channel. A QR code on the truck costs you nothing per order.</p>
<p style="margin-top:1rem"><strong>4. Over-prep and spoilage.</strong> Guessing volume leads to waste or sell-outs. Fix: watch what is actually being ordered in real time and prep to it; toggle items off the moment you run low so you are not disappointing paid customers.</p>
<p style="margin-top:1rem"><strong>5. Stale pricing.</strong> Truck menus are priced once and rarely revisited while beef, oil, and gas keep moving. Fix: re-cost your top sellers each season (the <a href="/resources/restaurant-food-cost-guide">food cost guide</a> walks through it).</p>
</div>
</div>

<div class="content-section">
<h2><i class="fas fa-list-ol"></i> Run your own numbers</h2>
<p class="section-intro">Five minutes with last month's takings tells you where your ceiling is.</p>
<ol class="step-list">
<li><div class="step-number">1</div><div class="step-content"><strong>Find your average order value</strong><p>Total takings divided by number of orders for a typical trading day.</p></div></li>
<li><div class="step-number">2</div><div class="step-content"><strong>Work out gross profit per order</strong><p>Average order value &times; (1 &minus; food cost %). At $18 and 34% food cost, that is about $11.88.</p></div></li>
<li><div class="step-number">3</div><div class="step-content"><strong>Estimate your peak throughput</strong><p>Count orders served in your busiest hour. That number, times the length of your rush, is your realistic ceiling.</p></div></li>
<li><div class="step-number">4</div><div class="step-content"><strong>Estimate walk-offs</strong><p>On a busy day, how many people approached, saw the line, and left? Even a rough count &times; gross profit per order is money on the table.</p></div></li>
<li><div class="step-number">5</div><div class="step-content"><strong>Compare the two levers</strong><p>Lifting price adds margin per order but can shrink volume. Lifting throughput (clearing the window) adds orders without touching price. For most trucks at peak, throughput is the bigger, safer lever.</p></div></li>
</ol>
</div>

<h2><i class="fas fa-circle-question"></i> Frequently asked questions</h2>
<div class="faq-item"><strong>Does order-ahead really change throughput?</strong><p>It moves the slow step, taking and confirming the order, off the service window and onto the customer's phone, before they reach you. The hatch is then free to do handover only, which is fast. You also cook to a visible queue instead of reacting to whoever is standing there.</p></div>
<div class="faq-item"><strong>Won't customers just leave if they see a wait time?</strong><p>Some will, but a stated wait is honest and lets people plan; a hidden wait behind a long line is what causes silent walk-offs. In practice a clear "12 min, order now" converts more of the people who would otherwise count the line and keep walking.</p></div>
<div class="faq-item"><strong>How is this different from the delivery apps?</strong><p>Aggregators take 25&ndash;35% of each order and own your customer. Order-ahead at the truck is your own channel: the customer scans a QR code, pays you, and you keep the relationship and the margin. QueueZero charges a flat subscription and 0% commission on orders.</p></div>
<div class="faq-item"><strong>Do I need new hardware?</strong><p>No. Customers order in their phone browser (no app). You run the kitchen display and now-serving board on a tablet or spare phone you already have. A printed QR sign is all that goes on the truck.</p></div>
<div class="faq-item"><strong>What about events and markets?</strong><p>Events are where queues and walk-offs are worst, and where order-ahead helps most: a captive crowd, a long line, and a short window. An event page can list every truck at the market with its live wait so customers spread their orders instead of bunching at one hatch.</p></div>
